# The Outstanding-Candidate Guide

*中文版见 [OUTSTANDING.zh.md](OUTSTANDING.zh.md) · companion to [PATTERNS.md](PATTERNS.md) & [PRINCIPLES.md](PRINCIPLES.md)*

Answer keys don't just give the solution — they editorialize about **what separates an outstanding
candidate from a competent one**. **33% of the 2,141 cases explicitly call this out** ("a strong
candidate…", "great candidates…"). This file aggregates every one of those signals into the behaviors
that actually move you from "solved it" to "hire". Percentages = share of cases whose guidance rewards
that behavior.

> The theme underneath all of them: **a good candidate answers the question asked; an outstanding one
> answers the question *behind* it, and the one the interviewer was *about* to ask.** That is second-order thinking.

---

## The headline: think one move ahead (second-order thinking)

Competent candidates compute a first-order answer ("raise price → +$42M"). Outstanding candidates
immediately ask **"…and then what happens?"** The keys reward, again and again, anticipating the
*consequence of the action* before being prompted. Combined, these second-order reflexes appear in a
large share of cases:

- **Anticipate the competitor's response (11%).** Any move you make, a rational rival reacts to. Raise price → do they hold and take your share, or follow? Enter → do they cut price or lock the channel? Name the reaction *and* your counter.
- **Check for cannibalization (10%).** A new product/channel/segment often eats your *existing* business. Always net it out: total revenue under the new option, not gross.
- **Trace knock-on / downstream effects (2% named explicitly, far more implicitly).** Cutting a cost may hurt quality → churn; a price rise may shrink the category; a layoff may hit morale → service. Follow the chain one link further than the obvious.
- **Stress-test the recommendation (19%).** State the *one assumption that, if wrong, breaks the answer* — "this works only if customers can absorb a 10% rise; below that, NPV flips." Then a mitigation.

**How to operationalize it:** after any first-order conclusion, say one sentence — *"The second-order effect to watch is [competitor / customer / cost / category] response, because…"* That single reflex is the most reliable "outstanding" signal in the corpus.

## The full ranking of outstanding behaviors (with how to do each)

### Tier 1 — the differentiators that show up most

**1. Surface the unprompted alternative (36% — the #1 signal).**
Almost every case has an option the interviewer doesn't volunteer: divest instead of fix, partner/license instead of build, do-nothing as a baseline, a different target. Outstanding candidates **raise the option the case didn't hand them** and weigh it.
→ *Do it:* before recommending, ask "what are the other ways to achieve the objective?" and name 1–2 the prompt didn't.

**2. Quantify the impact — put a number on every lever (21%).**
Don't say "we could raise price"; say "matching the competitor's price adds ~$42M, ~17% of profit." Magnitude turns a list into a decision.
→ *Do it:* every idea gets a rough size; rank by magnitude × feasibility, not by order you thought of them.

**3. Stress-test / name the breaking assumption + mitigation (19%).**
Commit, then bound it: the single risk that would flip the call, and how you'd de-risk it.
→ *Do it:* "My recommendation holds unless [X]; I'd validate [X] first."

### Tier 2 — rigor and judgment

**4. Be hypothesis-driven and visibly structured (15%).** Lead with where you think the answer is; let the structure serve the hypothesis, not a memorized template.
**5. Bring the customer / stakeholder lens (12%).** Numbers aren't enough — will customers accept the price? will employees execute? is there real demand/adoption? The human side is explicitly rewarded.
**6. Prioritize — find the biggest lever / 80-20 (11%).** Don't tackle the small easy win when a large structural one exists; say which lever matters most and why.
**7. Sanity-check every number (7%).** "That's ≈ [relatable magnitude] — feels right / off because…" Catching your own implausible result is a strong positive; freezing on an error is not.
**8. Tie back to the objective / synthesize "so what" (7%).** Every analysis step ends with its implication for the client's goal, not just a finding.
**9. Weigh explicit trade-offs (6%).** Name the tension (speed vs. control, margin vs. volume) rather than pretending an option is free.

### Tier 3 — behavioral tells of a top candidate

**10. Drive the case forward unprompted (6%).** Don't wait to be led — propose the next analysis, ask for the data you need, move toward the recommendation.
**11. State assumptions out loud (with a directional rationale) (2% named).** "I'll assume 5%, higher than the 3% average, because this segment is wealthier." Transparency > false precision.
**12. Show creativity / breadth in brainstorming (5%).** Volume *and* relevance; structured buckets, then a non-obvious idea or two.
**13. Balance qualitative with quantitative.** The best recommendations pair the number with the strategic/soft judgment that the number alone can't carry.

## The Outstanding 10 — a pre-case checklist

Run these reflexes in every case; each maps to a behavior the keys reward:

1. **"…and then what?"** — name the second-order effect of any move (competitor / customer / cost / category).
2. **"What's the alternative the interviewer hasn't mentioned?"** — surface it (divest / partner / do-nothing).
3. **Put a number on every lever** — and rank by magnitude.
4. **"What single assumption would break this?"** — state it + a mitigation.
5. **Lead with a hypothesis**, not a checklist.
6. **Ask the customer/people question** the math can't answer.
7. **Name the biggest lever** and go there first (80-20).
8. **Sanity-check** the answer to a relatable magnitude.
9. **Close every step with the "so what"** for the objective.
10. **Drive** — propose the next step before you're asked; end with recommendation + risk + next step.

## The one-sentence definition

> **Competent: solves the problem. Outstanding: solves it, anticipates its consequences, weighs the
> option nobody offered, sizes everything, names what would break the answer — and drives there without
> being led.**
